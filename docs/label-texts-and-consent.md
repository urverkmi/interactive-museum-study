# Label Texts & Consent Form

Nov 4, 2026 · @Michelle Chang

## How to use this doc

Versions B and C are written for all three works; version A is a word-for-word excerpt of each museum's own text, so the doc gives its exact boundaries and you paste it in from the linked page. Each work has the same structure: source, excerpt boundaries, fact list, three regions, then the B/C text.

**B and C share one text.** B shows the three headed segments on the page at once; C shows the same three segments, each revealed by selecting its region of the painting. Only the presentation differs, so any memory difference between B and C comes from the interaction, not the wording.

**Writing rules used for B and C** (dyslexia-friendly, after the British Dyslexia Association style guide): one idea per sentence, sentences under about 15 words, everyday words with any technical term explained once, active voice, no facts beyond those in A. For the developer: sans-serif font of at least 16 px, line spacing about 1.5, left-aligned text, no italics or underlining for emphasis, and an off-white background.

**Before data collection**

- [ ] Paste the three A excerpts and confirm each word count (120–200 words).
- [ ] Email each museum to say how its text and image are used, and ask for permission where noted below (Thyssen in particular).
- [ ] Fill the bracketed placeholders in the consent form.
- [ ] Write M1–M5 for each work from its fact list (Section 3 of the website spec).

## W1 Joachim Beuckelaer, Brothel Scene, 1563

**Source:** [KMSKA, “Brothel Scene”, collection page](https://kmska.be/en/masterpiece/brothel-scene), section “More about this work” (about 800 words). Image: free download from the same page; the work is listed as CC0 on the [Flemish Art Collection](https://vlaamsekunstcollectie.be/en/collection/858).

**Version A excerpt.** Start at the first sentence of “More about this work” (drunk people around a table). End after the ninth sentence, which says the man coming out of the back room shows the setting is a brothel, not a kitchen or inn. Stop before the sentence about the earlier version in the Walters Art Museum. Expected length is about 160 words.

**Fact list** (all appear in the excerpt)

| Fact | Content | Region |
| --- | --- | --- |
| F1 | Drunk people sit around a table and behave badly | R1 |
| F2 | The man at the table is fondling two women | R1 |
| F3 | A third woman stares into her tankard and forgets to feed her child | R1 |
| F4 | The man is of a better class and more smartly dressed | R1 |
| F5 | He drinks wine poured from a pewter flagon | R1 |
| F6 | The women are simply dressed and drink beer from earthenware jugs | R1 |
| F7 | The kitchen maid on the left is working hard and seems detached from the table | R2 |
| F8 | She looks out at the viewer with a doubtful expression | R2 |
| F9 | At the very back, an old man rests by the fire | R3 |
| F10 | A third man is coming out of the back room | R3 |
| F11 | That man is what identifies the setting as a brothel | R3 |
| F12 | Art historians used to think it was a kitchen or an inn | R3 |

**Regions:** R1 the group at the table (front, centre), R2 the kitchen maid (left), R3 the back of the room: the old man at the hearth and the doorway to the back room.

**Version B and C text**

*Around the table* (R1)

> A group of people sit around a table. They are clearly drunk and behaving badly. One man has his hands on two women. Another woman stares into her beer mug. She forgets to feed her child. The man is from a higher class than the women. His clothes are smarter. He drinks wine, poured from a pewter jug. The women wear simple clothes. They drink beer from clay jugs.

*The maid on the left* (R2)

> On the left, a kitchen maid is busy working. She seems to have no part in what happens at the table. She looks straight out at us. Her face shows doubt.

*At the back of the room* (R3)

> At the far back, an old man rests by the fire. Another man is coming out of a back room. This man tells us what kind of place this is: a brothel. Art historians used to think the scene showed a kitchen or an inn.

About 145 words. Every fact F1–F12 appears once; nothing is added.

## W2 Paul Gauguin, Coming and Going, Martinique, 1887

**Source:** [Museo Nacional Thyssen-Bornemisza, “Coming and Going, Martinique”, collection page](https://www.museothyssen.org/en/collection/artists/gauguin-paul/coming-and-going-martinique), text by Isabelle Cahn (five paragraphs, about 650 words). Carmen Thyssen Collection, inv. CTB.1979.88.

**Version A excerpt.** Take the third and fourth paragraphs whole: from the paragraph saying the exact site has not been identified, to the end of the paragraph whose last sentence says the path draws the eye to the fruit carriers. Expected length is about 195 words, near the upper limit, so check the count.

Why these paragraphs: the first two quote Gauguin's 1887 letter, which uses a racial term now considered offensive, and describe Martinican women through a colonial lens. Paragraphs 3–4 avoid that language and still mention three distinct parts of the painting.

**Fact list**

| Fact | Content | Region |
| --- | --- | --- |
| F1 | Gauguin had made sketches and pastels of people and animals | R1 |
| F2 | He added them later to the landscape | R1 |
| F3 | The landscape was probably painted in his studio | R1 |
| F4 | This re-composing distanced him from the Impressionists' naturalism | R1 |
| F5 | It produced a space without depth, typical of Japanese prints | R1 |
| F6 | Men, women, goats, sheep and hens by the road take up little space; the painting is mainly landscape | R1 |
| F7 | The vegetation is shown as large masses, like a tapestry of dark, intense colours | R2 |
| F8 | Vermilion (bright red) patches appear among the greens | R2 |
| F9 | He used oblique brushstrokes typical of Cézanne, giving structure and a vibrant surface | R2 |
| F10 | The laterite path crosses the meadow and draws the eye to the fruit carriers | R3 |
| F11 | The fruit carriers are the main subject | R3 |
| F12 | The exact site has not been identified | R3 |
| F13 | It is probably built from real elements, perhaps seen on the property where Gauguin lived | R3 |

**Regions:** R1 the figures and animals by the roadside, R2 the masses of trees and plants, R3 the path and the fruit carriers it leads to.

**Version B and C text**

*The people and animals* (R1)

> By the side of the road are men, women, goats, sheep and hens. They take up only a small part of the painting. It is mostly a landscape. Gauguin first drew these people and animals in sketches and pastels. Later he added them to the landscape. He probably painted the landscape in his studio. Building the picture this way took him away from the realism of the Impressionists. It also made the space look flat, with no depth, like a Japanese print.

*The trees and plants* (R2)

> The plants are painted as large masses of colour. Together they look like a tapestry of dark, strong colours. Patches of bright red stand out among the greens. Gauguin used slanting brushstrokes, like the painter Cézanne. They give the shapes structure and make the surface look alive.

*The path* (R3)

> A path of laterite, a kind of red earth, crosses the meadow. It leads your eye to the people carrying fruit. These fruit carriers are the main subject of the painting. Nobody knows exactly where this place is. It was probably put together from real things, perhaps seen where Gauguin lived.

About 180 words. Every fact F1–F13 appears once; nothing is added. Avoid questions on the path's colour, since “red earth” only explains the word laterite.

## W3 Alphonse Mucha, The Abolition of Serfdom in Russia, 1914

**Source:** [Prague City Gallery, “The Abolition of Serfdom in Russia 1861”, Google Arts & Culture story](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/bQWxCBI4IULgUg). Prague City Gallery holds the Slav Epic for the City of Prague. The story is written from the 2022 exhibition catalogue of the Municipal Cultural Centre of Moravský Krumlov (text by Pavla Červinková).

**Version A excerpt.** Start at the sentence describing a cold March afternoon on Red Square. End with the last sentence of the story, about why Mucha dropped his plan to show a glorious event. The story is split into captions; join them with single spaces. Expected length is about 190 words.

Two things to know. The source has a grammar slip in the sentence about the round platform; keep it, because A must be word for word. The [Moravský Krumlov site](https://www.mucha-epopej.cz/the-slav-epic/the-abolition-of-serfdom-in-russia/) has the same text but says “morning”, so do not write a question on the time of day. The [Mucha Foundation text](https://muchafoundation.org/gallery/browse-works/object/228) should not go in *Learn more*: it repeats facts from A (the fog, the sun, the cathedral), and the spec keeps question facts out of that overlay.

**Fact list**

| Fact | Content | Region |
| --- | --- | --- |
| F1 | The scene is Red Square in Moscow, in front of Saint Basil's Cathedral | R1 |
| F2 | The cathedral was built by Ivan the Terrible in the 16th century | R1 |
| F3 | The Kremlin towers rise in the background | R1 |
| F4 | Above the cathedral, the first rays of sun break through thick fog | R1 |
| F5 | The sun's rays symbolise dawn and freedom | R1 |
| F6 | A round platform stands to the right of the cathedral | R2 |
| F7 | From it, the Tsar's order abolishing serfdom was announced on 5 March 1861 | R2 |
| F8 | The officials are leaving it after the announcement | R2 |
| F9 | It is a cold March afternoon | R3 |
| F10 | The rural people stay in the square; each takes the new freedom in their own way | R3 |
| F11 | Some are happy about new opportunities, others do not know what to do | R3 |
| F12 | Mucha painted it in 1914–1915, after a study trip to Russia in 1913 | R3 |
| F13 | He dropped his plan to show a glorious event after learning of ordinary Russians' oppression and real living standard | R3 |

**Regions:** R1 the cathedral, the Kremlin towers and the sun in the fog, R2 the round platform with the officials (right of the cathedral), R3 the people standing in the square.

**Version B and C text**

*The cathedral and the sky* (R1)

> The scene is Red Square in Moscow, in front of Saint Basil's Cathedral. Ivan the Terrible had the cathedral built in the 16th century. Behind it rise the towers of the Kremlin. Above the cathedral, the first rays of sun push through thick fog. The sunlight stands for dawn and freedom.

*The platform* (R2)

> To the right of the cathedral is a round platform. From here, the Tsar's order to end serfdom was read out on 5 March 1861. The reading is over. The officials are leaving the platform.

*The people in the square* (R3)

> It is a cold March afternoon. People from the countryside stay in the square. Their faces show that each of them takes the new freedom in their own way. Some are glad about new chances. Others do not know what to do. Mucha painted this in 1914–1915, after a study trip to Russia in 1913. At first he wanted to show the end of serfdom as a glorious event. He changed his plan when he learned how ordinary Russians were oppressed, and how poorly they lived.

About 175 words. Every fact F1–F13 appears once; nothing is added.

## Consent form

The text below is the full participant information, shown on its own page and linked from the guide page. Bracketed items need filling in; the legal basis and archiving lines follow common practice at Swedish universities, so check them with your data protection officer.

---

### Participant information: Reading about artworks online

**Who we are.** This study is run by Yuxin Zhang and Michelle Chang at KTH Royal Institute of Technology in Stockholm, Sweden. Contact: yuxz@kth.se, michang@kth.se. The data controller is KTH Royal Institute of Technology.

**What the study is about.** We are studying how the way a museum text is written affects what people remember from it and how they experience reading it. The results will help museums write texts that work for more visitors.

**What you will do.** You will see three paintings from museum collections, one after another, each with a short text. After each one you will answer a few questions about what you remember and how easy the text was. At the end there are four short questions. It takes about 12–15 minutes on a laptop or desktop computer. Please do not take notes, take screenshots or look anything up.

**Content note.** One painting, from 1563, shows a tavern and brothel scene with drinking and sexual behaviour. Nothing in it is explicit.

**Who can take part.** Anyone aged 18 or over who can read English. You do not need to know anything about art.

**Taking part is voluntary.** You can stop at any time by closing the window, without giving a reason. Answers given up to that point are kept as an incomplete session and are only counted as a dropout. If you finish and later want your answers removed, email us the participant code shown on the last page. Once the data has been analysed and published, removal may no longer be possible.

**What we record**

| What | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Whether English is your first language and, if not, how well you read it | To describe who took part |
| Your answers to the memory questions, ratings and short written comments | To compare the texts |
| How long each page was open, and when the page was hidden or in the background | To measure reading time and spot interruptions |
| What you clicked: *Learn more*, and the parts of a painting you selected | To see how people use the texts |
| Device type, window size and browser | To check that the study worked on your screen |
| The date and time of each step | To link the steps above in order |

We do not ask for your name. Your answers are stored under a random code. We do not store your IP address. Please do not write your name or other personal details in the comment boxes.

**Optional interview.** At the end you can leave an email address if you would like to take part in a 20-minute online interview. Your email is stored separately and cannot be linked to your answers. We delete it when the interviews are finished, by December 16th 2026. If you are interviewed, you will be asked for consent again.

**How we use and keep the data.** Only the research team can see the raw data. It is stored within the EU. We publish results only as group summaries. Short comments may be quoted, without anything that could identify you. The raw data is not shared outside the research team.

**Legal basis and your rights.** We process your data as research in the public interest (GDPR Article 6(1)(e)). You have the right to ask what data we hold about you, to have it corrected or deleted, and to complain to the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection (IMY, imy.se). This study does not collect sensitive personal data and carries no more than minimal risk, so it does not require review under the Swedish Ethical Review Act (2003:460). 

**The artworks and texts.** The paintings belong to the KMSKA in Antwerp, the Carmen Thyssen Collection in Madrid and the City of Prague. One text for each painting is taken word for word from that museum's website. The other texts are our own simplified versions, written from the museum texts; the museums did not write or approve them. The paintings show their own times, including a colonial-era view of Martinique and the end of serfdom in Russia; we show them for research, not to endorse those views. Full credits are on the Sources page.

**Use of AI.** The simplified texts were first drafted with the help of an AI system (Claude) and then checked and edited by the research team against the museum texts.

**Questions.** Contact Michelle (michang@kth.se) or Yuxin (yuxz@kth.se). You can save or print this page.

## Putting it into the site

The participant information goes on its own page, opened from a link on the guide page; the three consent boxes sit on the guide page itself, and *Start* stays disabled until all three are ticked. Opening the information in a new tab keeps the guide page, and its ticked boxes, in place.

Two small additions to the spec follow from the consent text: store a `consent_version` with `consent_ts`, so you know which wording each person agreed to, and show the `participant_id` on the debrief page, since that code is how people withdraw.

**Instruction for Claude Code.** First export this doc as Markdown and save it in the repo as `content/label-texts-and-consent.md`, after pasting the three A excerpts into it. Then paste:

```markdown
Read docs/study-website-spec.md and content/label-texts-and-consent.md.

1. Split the content file into: content/participant-info.md (the participant information), content/sources.md (the Sources page), and one content file per artwork holding version A text and the three B/C segments (heading, region id, body). Keep the wording exactly.
2. Add /participant-info and /sources pages that render those files; link both from the footer.
3. On the guide page, add a link "Read the full participant information" that opens /participant-info in a new tab, and the three consent checkboxes. Keep Start disabled until all three are ticked. On Start, save consent_ts and consent_version.
4. Version B shows the three segments with their headings. Version C shows the same segments, each revealed when its region is selected.
5. Show the same credit line under each painting in all versions.
6. Show the participant_id on the debrief page as "Your participant code".
```
