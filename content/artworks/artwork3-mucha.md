# W3 — Alphonse Mucha, The Abolition of Serfdom in Russia, 1914

## Version A (museum's original text, word for word)

The painting depicts a cold March afternoon in the Red Square in Moscow, in front of Saint Basil’s Cathedral. The towers of the Kremlin rise in the background. On the right of the cathedral — built by Ivan the Terrible in the 16th century — there is a round platform which the Tsar’s order to abolish serfdom was announced on 5 March 1861. After the announcement it is being left by the officials. The rural people stay in the square and their faces show that each of them perceives their acquired freedom in his/her own way. Some of them are happy about new opportunities… while others do not know what to do. Above the towers of Saint Basil’s Cathedral, the first rays of the sun, symbolising dawn and freedom, are gradually forcing their way through the thick fog. Alphonse Mucha created this painting in 1914-1915, having returned from his 1913 study trip to Russia. He changed his original intention to paint the abolition of serfdom in Russia as a glorious historic event because he had learned about the oppression and the real living standard of the ordinary Russian people.


## Version B/C segments

### The cathedral and the sky (R1)

The scene is Red Square in Moscow, in front of Saint Basil's Cathedral. Ivan the Terrible had the cathedral built in the 16th century. Behind it rise the towers of the Kremlin. Above the cathedral, the first rays of sun push through thick fog. The sunlight stands for dawn and freedom.

### The platform (R2)

To the right of the cathedral is a round platform. From here, the Tsar's order to end serfdom was read out on 5 March 1861. The reading is over. The officials are leaving the platform.

### The people in the square (R3)

It is a cold March afternoon. People from the countryside stay in the square. Their faces show that each of them takes the new freedom in their own way. Some are glad about new chances. Others do not know what to do. Mucha painted this in 1914–1915, after a study trip to Russia in 1913. At first he wanted to show the end of serfdom as a glorious event. He changed his plan when he learned how ordinary Russians were oppressed, and how poorly they lived.
